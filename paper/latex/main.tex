\begin{abstract}
Latent objectives are common in real operational settings: stakeholders reveal
constraints, preferences, and feedback over time, while the true utility
function governing good decisions may remain implicit or change without
announcement. We introduce \textbf{LatentGoalOps}, a benchmark for evaluating
latent objective inference and adaptation under silent objective shifts. Agents
observe a fixed public operating schema consisting of business state,
stakeholder messages, decision ledgers, delayed effects, and governance
constraints, but never observe the hidden objective used by the deterministic
grader. The benchmark contains three sequential business-operations
environments; our main analysis focuses on two week-long anchor tasks, with a
shorter planning task used as breadth evidence. Across the anchor tasks,
restricted-view leakage audits show no easy shortcut, models incur a persistent
penalty on silently shifted episodes, and the best current local model remains
roughly 0.20 below a myopic oracle that observes the active hidden objective
but does not solve the full future trajectory. We further find that performance
is mechanism-sensitive: several sharp heldout drops are caused by
structured-action failures rather than latent-policy quality alone. For this
reason, LatentGoalOps reports strict score, native-only score, and interface
reliability jointly. These results position LatentGoalOps as a benchmark for
studying hidden-objective adaptation under a fixed public schema and
deterministic grader, rather than as a measure of general business-agent
readiness. Small hosted-model probes show heterogeneous interface reliability,
but none closes the oracle gap.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Many agent benchmarks make the objective explicit: the instruction is written
down, the tool contract is visible, and success is defined against an external
goal. Recent suites such as \textsc{$\tau$}-bench and BrowserGym have pushed
that setting toward longer-horizon, tool-using evaluation in realistic
interfaces~\citep{taubench,browsergym}. Real operating environments, however,
often require a different capability. Leadership priorities are not always
fully stated, stakeholder messages are noisy, visible KPI movement is
ambiguous, and the underlying objective can change without announcement.

This paper targets that gap. We study \emph{latent objective inference and
adaptation}: inferring an unstated reward structure from public evidence,
acting coherently through a strict interface, and adapting when the hidden
objective changes mid-episode. We instantiate this construct in
\textbf{LatentGoalOps}, a synthetic but structured operating-world benchmark
with persistent entities, delayed effects, deterministic grading, and silent
objective shifts. The synthetic construction is intentional: it gives the
benchmark hidden-objective ground truth while preserving a realistic operating
surface for the agent.

The release contains three paper-facing sequential environments:
\emph{P1 Startup Week}, \emph{P2 Incident Response Week}, and
\emph{P3 Quarterly Headcount Plan}. The paper centers P1 and P2 as anchor
tasks; P3 serves only as shorter breadth evidence. Within that scope, the
frozen evidence supports three claims: current local open-weight
configurations retain a clear myopic-oracle gap, within-family behavior is
heterogeneous rather than uniformly monotone, and some large heldout drops are
interface-mediated rather than pure policy regressions.

\paragraph{Related work and positioning.}
Existing interactive benchmarks such as \textsc{$\tau$}-bench and BrowserGym
primarily evaluate explicit user goals in realistic interfaces, while
structured-output and function-calling evaluations such as
JSONSchemaBench and BFCL isolate interface control itself
~\citep{taubench,browsergym,jsonschemabench,bfcl}. LatentGoalOps instead hides
the objective, introduces delayed effects and silent shifts, and requires
strict structured actions under non-stationarity. Following recent
benchmark-methodology work, we therefore report strict score, native-only
score, and interface reliability together rather than a single leaderboard
scalar~\citep{agentrewardbench,betterbench,dataperf,benchmarkrepos}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item We introduce LatentGoalOps, a benchmark suite for latent objective
  inference and adaptation under silent non-stationarity with delayed effects,
  hidden strategic objectives, deterministic grading, two week-long anchor
  environments, and one shorter breadth task.
  \item We release a runnable benchmark artifact with a frozen protocol,
  core and heldout splits, deterministic baselines, restricted-view leakage
  probes, generated paper assets, and reviewer-facing reproducibility
  documentation.
  \item We report a strengthened local benchmark matrix with 630 primary model
  episodes and 360 baseline episodes, plus a P1/P2 anchor-task robustness
  slice that isolates the benchmark's strongest latent-adaptation settings.
  \item We add exploratory hosted-model probes for Gemini 3 Flash preview,
  GPT-5.4 mini, and Claude Sonnet 4.6 to test whether accessible API models
  immediately close the myopic-oracle gap under the same action shell.
  \item We show that the benchmark's empirical story is mechanism-sensitive:
  some larger-model changes are native-policy movements, while some of the
  sharpest heldout regressions are interface-mediated drops.
\end{itemize}

\section{Benchmark}

\subsection{Environment Loop}

Each episode is a finite partially observed decision process. At step $t$, the
agent receives a public observation $o_t$, emits a structured action $a_t$, and
the environment advances to the next state. Public observations include inbox
messages, KPI dashboards, dated decision ledgers, pending effects, realized
effects, customer accounts, stakeholder personas, team state, market context,
and visible governance constraints. The hidden objective $g_t$ is never shown
directly.

Formally, an episode is a trajectory
\[
\tau = (o_0, a_0, o_1, a_1, \ldots, o_T),
\]
with hidden state $x_t$, public observation $o_t = \psi(x_t)$, and active
hidden objective state $g_t$. The environment maintains a normalized
four-channel metric vector $m_t \in [0,1]^4$ over growth, retention, revenue,
and efficiency, and an active latent weight vector $w_t \in \Delta^4$. The
latent utility used by the grader is
\[
U_t = \mathrm{clip}_{[0,1]} \left( \sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} m_{t,c} \, w_{t,c} \right),
\qquad
\mathcal{C} = \{\text{growth}, \text{retention}, \text{revenue}, \text{efficiency}\}.
\]

The action interface is intentionally strict. On the sequential tasks, a valid
action is a JSON-serializable payload over task-specific fields such as
\texttt{chosen\_initiatives}, \texttt{messaging\_action},
\texttt{pricing\_change\_pct}, \texttt{support\_policy}, or
\texttt{budget\_allocations}. If the model fails to emit a valid structured
action, the benchmark executes a fallback action and the trajectory still
advances. In other words, interface failures are part of the task, not merely
parser trivia. This follows the broader lesson from structured-generation and
tool-use evaluation that output control is part of agent capability rather than
an implementation footnote~\citep{jsonschemabench,bfcl}.

\subsection{Three Sequential Environments and Silent Shifts}

LatentGoalOps evaluates three sequential operating environments. The
implementation preserves internal task ids for reproducibility, but the main
paper uses the cleaner P1--P3 names throughout. Table~\ref{tab:tasksuite}
summarizes the benchmark environments and their roles in the manuscript.

P1 Startup Week is a ten-step operating week with initiatives, renewals, and
delayed business consequences. P2 Incident Response Week is a seven-step
incident setting where trust repair, governance, recovery speed, and cost can
conflict. P3 Quarterly Headcount Plan is a shorter staffing task used only for
breadth. Heldout evaluation changes scenario families and delayed-effect
realizations while preserving the same public schema and grading semantics.
Silent shifts occur with probability $0.6$, usually at steps $\{3,4,5\}$, and
can alter latent weights as well as risk posture, planning horizon, segment
focus, and governance strictness. The model is never told that the shift
occurred.

\subsection{Scoring and Reporting}

For the primary sequential tasks, the episode score is an explicit weighted
combination of final latent utility $U$, adaptation $A$, coherence $C$, and
constraint satisfaction $K$:
\[
S_{\mathrm{task}} = \alpha_U U + \alpha_A A + \alpha_C C + \alpha_K K.
\]
Task-specific coefficients and no-shift renormalization rules are fixed in the
release protocol. This keeps the main paper focused on
the measurement construct rather than coefficient bookkeeping.

For \emph{Startup Week} and \emph{Incident Response Week}, adaptation measures
how quickly post-shift decisions improve under the active hidden objective and
whether that recovery is sustained; P3 uses the same principle with a shorter
horizon. We report overall strict score, native-only score, shifted versus
unshifted score, and interface reliability. Release aggregates pool 10 seeds
per task across P1--P3, while P1/P2 anchor slices isolate the strongest
construct signal; aggregate views use 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals.

\section{Evaluation and Benchmark Validity}

\subsection{Baseline Separation}

LatentGoalOps has meaningful score dynamic range on the frozen release
aggregate over P1--P3. Random, visible-heuristic, and myopic-oracle baselines
preserve the expected ordering on both splits, and the myopic-oracle gap
remains substantial on both splits. The myopic oracle
observes the active hidden objective and chooses the best immediate action, but
does not solve the full future trajectory; we use it as a construct headroom
reference rather than as a fair agent baseline or human proxy.

\subsection{Leakage Audit}

Restricted-view centroid probes test for shortcut access to the hidden
objective. Table~\ref{tab:leakagecompact} shows that P1 and P2 stay near the
four-way chance regime under both full and restricted views, with
\texttt{explicit\_goal\_token\_rate} equal to 0.0 throughout. P3 is less
clean: its core restricted-view maximum reaches 0.35, which is why we treat it
as breadth evidence rather than as a validity anchor. Each probe slice uses 20
train and 20 test examples, so this audit is a shortcut screen rather than a
proof of impossibility.

Utility-only rescoring sharply compresses the myopic-oracle gap, which is the
expected pattern if the benchmark is measuring latent-objective adaptation
rather than only final-state utility.

\section{Main Results}

\subsection{The Two Week-Long Anchor Tasks Carry the Main Result}

Because P3 is shorter-horizon and less discriminative than the two week-long
environments, Table~\ref{tab:anchorslice} reports a P1/P2-only robustness
slice computed directly from the frozen release as the paper's main result
view. On the anchor slice, the strongest local core mean is Qwen
27B at 0.425 and the strongest heldout mean is DeepSeek 8B at 0.431, while the
myopic-oracle anchor means are 0.626 on core and 0.630 on heldout. The best
local anchor results therefore still trail the oracle by about 0.20.

\input{generated/anchor_slice_table.tex}

Taken together with the leakage audit and the persistent shifted-versus-
unshifted gap, the P1/P2 anchor tasks make the intended construct visible:
public trajectory evidence is informative but incomplete, so successful
behavior requires latent-goal tracking rather than shortcut extraction.

\subsection{The Full Release Aggregate Preserves the Same Story}

The full P1--P3 aggregate preserves the same story: the strongest frozen local
aggregates are 0.443 on core and 0.446 on heldout, still about 0.15 below the
myopic-oracle baselines of 0.599 and 0.598.

\subsection{Hosted API Probes Improve Reliability but Do Not Close the Gap}

To test whether readily accessible hosted models close the benchmark's oracle
gap, we ran small OpenRouter probes on the P1--P3 aggregate after freezing the primary
local-model matrix. These runs are exploratory and not used as load-bearing
within-family scaling evidence, but they answer a practical reviewer question:
whether modern API models immediately erase the oracle gap. Because P1 and P2
are the load-bearing anchor tasks, Table~\ref{tab:apiprobes} reports both
P1/P2 means and the broader P1--P3 aggregate.

\input{generated/api_probe_table.tex}

The probes separate interface reliability from latent-objective quality.
Gemini 3 Flash preview often produced plausible intent with non-native field
names, yielding low native rates and many empty fallbacks. GPT-5.4 mini was
mostly native and reached 0.388/0.352 on the P1/P2 core/heldout anchor slice
(0.418/0.381 on P1--P3). Claude Sonnet 4.6 was the cleanest probe, with native
rate 1.00 on both splits and P1/P2 scores of 0.423/0.385. None of these
descriptive point estimates closes the myopic-oracle gap. Under these small
probes, better shell reliability does not by itself close the gap to a
privileged policy that sees the active hidden objective.

\subsection{Some of the Sharpest Heldout Drops Are Interface-Mediated}

Across the frozen release, scaling is family-specific rather than monotone, but
the most important mechanism is interface confounding rather than raw ranking
alone. DeepSeek remains the cleanest native-policy signal: its three reported
sizes stay at native rate 1.0 on both splits, so its gains and losses are best
read as policy movement. Qwen 2B $\rightarrow$ 9B also changes at native rate
1.0, giving a clean example of non-monotone native-policy behavior. The sharp
heldout failures, by contrast, are dominated by shell fragility.

\subsection{Silent Shifts Remain the Dominant Stressor}

Silent objective shifts remain the primary benchmark stress test. Across the
release matrix, shifted episodes are materially harder than unshifted ones on
both core and heldout, with a median unshifted-minus-shifted gap of 0.172 in
the release-wide diagnostics. This gap is not explained away by shell failures:
models with perfect native-action reliability, including all DeepSeek sizes,
still pay a substantial shift penalty. That is the benchmark construct we most
want to isolate. Figure~\ref{fig:shifted} makes this visible at the aggregate
level.

\section{Discussion}

LatentGoalOps clears the benchmark-science checks we care about most: baseline
ordering, a persistent oracle gap, a consistent shift penalty, and no easy
shortcut on the two anchor tasks. Because malformed actions change the
trajectory itself, strict score, native-only score, and native episode rate
need to be reported together.

\section{Limitations and Intended Use}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item The benchmark is synthetic and intended for comparative evaluation, not
  operational advice or direct real-world deployment claims.
  \item The heldout split preserves schema and grader semantics, and P3 is
  treated as shorter planning breadth rather than as a load-bearing anchor.
  \item GPT-OSS 120B is API-served, and this version does not include human
  baselines or a full human-audited grader validation study.
  \item The hosted-model probes are intentionally small because of API access,
  budget, and deadline constraints. They should be read as exploratory
  external probes, not exhaustive frontier-model coverage.
  \item The adaptation metric uses thresholded detection rules; we expose and
  audit those choices in the release protocol, but smoother alternatives remain
  possible.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

LatentGoalOps isolates hidden-objective adaptation under silent
non-stationarity. On the tighter P1/P2 anchor slice and the broader release
aggregate, the benchmark shows no easy shortcut on the anchor tasks, a
persistent shift penalty, and a clear gap between current local models and the
myopic-oracle reference, while also revealing that some sharp heldout failures
are interface-mediated. That combination argues for evaluating latent policy
quality and action-shell reliability separately.
